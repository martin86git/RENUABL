# RENUABL phone assistant: solar sales knowledge

Facts as at 8 November 2026. For the RENUABL phone assistant (Revo) talking to Victorian homeowners about solar, batteries and EV chargers.

---

## 1. How to use this knowledge

- These facts help you explain things. They never override your call rules: you are an automated assistant, the call is recorded, and you never change a price, offer a discount, promise a date, rebate, approval or saving, or agree to anything on the phone.
- Anything about **this customer's** system, price, rebates or savings comes only from their own proposal or details. Never work out their figures from the general numbers here. The examples below are illustrations, not quotes.
- If a question is specific to their home and the answer isn't in their proposal, say the team will confirm it, and offer a call back.
- Keep answers short and spoken: one idea at a time, plain words, then check they're with you.
- Customers are sold only by signing the proposal online. Your job is to answer questions, deal with worries, and guide them to the proposal page when they're ready. Suggest going ahead at most once.

## 2. Who RENUABL is and how it works

- RENUABL helps Victorian homeowners get solar, batteries and EV chargers. We recommend one system sized from the home's electricity bill, not a catalogue to choose from.
- The installers we work with are called **installation partners**. In Victoria the main installation partner is Primero Electric & Solar.
- The steps:
  1. The customer gives us their address and their latest electricity bill, or a rough idea of what they spend.
  2. Our team designs a system for their roof from satellite imagery and the bill.
  3. They get a proposal: the system, the price after rebates and the estimated savings.
  4. There's a short call to go through it and answer questions. Anything still to confirm (switchboard, access, shade) is checked before installation.
  5. They go ahead by signing the proposal online. Nothing is final or charged until then.
  6. The installation partner installs the system, usually in one day. They arrive between 7 and 9am on a weekday.
  7. The network and the electricity retailer finish the grid connection, and the system starts exporting.
- An **indicative proposal** is a first look, built before every detail is checked. It can't be signed. The next step is a chat or a site check, then a final proposal.
- If they used a spend range instead of a bill, every figure is indicative and gets confirmed from the bill later. Never call it as accurate as a bill.

## 3. Solar basics

**kW and kWh**
- kW (kilowatt) is the size of a system: how much power it can make at once. A "6.6 kW system" is about fourteen 475-watt panels.
- kWh (kilowatt-hour) is energy over time. It's what the bill charges for and what a battery stores.

**How much a system makes**
- In Melbourne, each kW of panels makes about 3.8 kWh a day averaged over the year. A summer day makes much more, a winter day about half the average.
- So a 6.6 kW system makes roughly 25 kWh on an average day. That's a rule of thumb: the design for their roof uses its own direction, pitch and shade.

**Using solar as it's made**
- A home uses its solar as it's made: the fridge, appliances, working from home. As a rule of thumb that's 30 to 40% of what the panels make.
- The rest goes to the grid for a small feed-in credit, or into a battery if there is one.
- Solar is worth most when it's used at home, because each kWh used is one not bought at the full price (often 25 to 40 cents).
- Running the dishwasher, washing machine, pool pump or hot water during the day uses more of the solar.

**Roof direction and shade**
- North-facing roofs make the most over a year. East and west make a little less, and spread the output across the morning and afternoon, which can suit a home well.
- South-facing roofs make noticeably less and are used only when needed.
- Shade from trees, chimneys or neighbouring buildings cuts output. The design is checked for it, and the site check before installation confirms it.

**Roof types**
- Tin (Colorbond) roofs: panels on rails fixed with tin feet.
- Tiled roofs: tile hooks go under the tiles to hold the rails.
- Klip-lok and other flat metal roofs: clamps that grip the roof's ribs.
- Flat roofs: panels can lie flat (low profile, a little less output) or sit on tilt frames angled 10 to 15 degrees. Frames give more output but need more room and can be seen from the street.

**Inverters**
- The inverter turns the panels' DC power into the AC power the home uses. Every RENUABL system comes with a new inverter.
- A **string inverter** is for solar without a battery.
- A **hybrid inverter** runs solar and a battery together.
- Panels can be up to 133% of the inverter's rating (up to 150% with a hybrid and a battery). That's normal and allowed: panels rarely make their full rating at once.

**Single phase and three phase**
- Most homes have single-phase power. Some larger or newer homes have three-phase power.
- Three-phase homes get a three-phase inverter.
- It's on the switchboard and sometimes the bill, and it's confirmed before installation.

**How long it lasts**
- Panels usually carry a 25-year product warranty and keep making power for decades, slowly losing a little output each year.
- Inverters usually last 10 to 15 years.
- Panels need little upkeep. Rain cleans them, and a check now and then for damage or heavy dirt is enough.

## 4. Batteries

**What a battery does**
- It stores the spare solar from the day so the home can use it in the evening and overnight, instead of buying power from the grid at full price.
- It charges from the solar first. The home uses it once the sun goes down.

**Nominal and usable**
- A battery's size is its full (nominal) capacity. RENUABL sizes batteries assuming about 90% is usable.

**How the battery is sized**
- From the home's evening and overnight use on the bill.
- The solar is sized so a winter day's spare can still fill the battery, so the system works through the year, not just in summer.
- Sigenergy batteries come in 8 kWh modules, so the battery can grow later by adding modules (subject to the installation partner and network).

**Blackouts**
- A battery doesn't power the home in a blackout unless backup is wired in. Blackout backup is an optional extra.
- With backup, the battery keeps chosen circuits running, such as lights, the fridge, the internet and some power points. Which circuits is agreed with the customer.
- Never say a battery will run the whole home in a blackout.

**Is a battery worth it?**
- It depends on how much the home uses after dark and how much solar it exports. A home that's out all day and busy in the evening gets the most from one.
- Use only the figures on the customer's proposal. If their proposal has a system without a battery as well, it's fine to compare the two.
- Federal battery rebates have made batteries much more affordable since July 2025 (see section 7).

**Already have solar?**
- A battery can usually be added to existing solar. The existing inverter decides how: it's confirmed from a photo of the inverter or on site.
- If the bill shows exports, the battery can be sized from them: daily exports are the export kWh divided by the days in the billing period.

**Lifespan**
- Home batteries usually carry a 10-year warranty, often with a minimum capacity at the end of it.

## 5. EV chargers

- A smart EV charger (RENUABL uses Sigenergy's 7 kW AC charger) can charge the car from spare solar during the day, and from the grid or battery when needed.
- At 7 kW it adds roughly 40 to 50 km of driving range an hour, depending on the car.
- Charging an EV mostly from solar can greatly cut the cost of driving compared with petrol. The actual saving depends on how far they drive and when the car is home.
- It's included in the "Maximum" option, or can be added.

## 7. Rebates and incentives

Rebates change. Give the customer the figures on their own proposal, and point them to the official sites for the rules: the Clean Energy Regulator (cer.gov.au) and Solar Victoria (solar.vic.gov.au).

### 7.1 STCs for solar: the Small-scale Renewable Energy Scheme (federal)

**What it is**
- A federal scheme that creates certificates, called STCs (small-scale technology certificates), for new solar systems up to 100 kW.
- The installer or retailer usually takes the certificates and gives the customer the value as a discount on the price. That's why it shows as a line off the price, not a cheque.

**How many certificates**
- System size (kW) × the postcode's zone rating × the "deeming" years left in the scheme.
- Most of Victoria, including Melbourne, is zone 4 (1.185). Much of northern and western regional Victoria is zone 3 (1.382), which gets more.
- The scheme ends in 2030, so deeming years fall each year: 5 for a system installed in 2026, 4 in 2027, then 3, 2 and 1.
- So the solar rebate gets smaller each 1 January. That's a fact you can share. Never use it to pressure anyone.

**What a certificate is worth**
- The government sets no price. Certificates trade on a market, recently around $35 to $40 each.

**Example (an illustration only):** a 6.65 kW system in Melbourne installed in 2026 is 6.65 × 1.185 × 5 ≈ 39 certificates, around $1,500 at $38 each.

**Rules**
- The system must use approved products and be installed by an accredited installer. RENUABL's systems are designed for that.

### 7.2 STCs for batteries: the Cheaper Home Batteries Program (federal)

**What it is**
- Since 1 July 2025, home batteries installed with solar (new or existing) earn certificates too, through the same scheme.
- The value is given as a discount on the price, the same way.

**How many certificates**
- Usable battery kWh × a factor set for the installation date. The factor steps down every six months until the program ends in 2030:
  - 6.8 from 1 May to 31 December 2026
  - 5.7 in the first half of 2027
  - 5.2 in the second half of 2027
  - then lower again each half year.
- Capacity is counted in full up to 14 kWh, at 60% from 14 to 28 kWh, and at 15% from 28 to 50 kWh. Nothing counts above 50 kWh.

**Example (an illustration only):** a 16 kWh battery installed in late 2026 counts as 14 + (2 × 0.6) = 15.2 kWh. 15.2 × 6.8 ≈ 103 certificates, around $3,900 at $38 each.

**Rules**
- The battery must be an approved product, able to join a virtual power plant (it doesn't have to join one), and installed by an accredited installer.

### 7.3 Solar Victoria (Victorian government)

**Solar panel rebate**
- Up to $1,400 off a new solar system, and never more than half the solar cost after STCs.
- For eligible owner-occupiers. The main criteria:
  - they own and live in the home
  - combined household taxable income under the limit
  - home valued under $3 million
  - no solar at the address and no Solar Victoria rebate in recent years.
- Solar Victoria decides eligibility. Send them to solar.vic.gov.au/solar-panel-rebate to check.
- It's for new solar systems only, not for adding panels or a battery to existing solar.
- The customer applies themselves online, using a quote from an authorised retailer. Approval usually takes 7 to 10 business days.
- That's why install dates for these systems start about 14 days out, and are "subject to your rebate approval".

**Interest-free loan**
- Up to the rebate amount (up to $1,400), paid back over 4 years.
- It lowers the upfront cost, not the price.
- Only with the rebate.

**Battery rebate**
- Solar Victoria's battery rebate isn't currently offered. The federal battery certificates (7.2) are the battery incentive now.
- Don't promise a state battery rebate.

**How to talk about it**
- Always say "if eligible". Never say a customer will definitely get it.

### 7.4 Feed-in tariffs

- The feed-in tariff is the credit the electricity retailer pays for each kWh of solar sent to the grid.
- In Victoria, the Essential Services Commission sets a minimum each year, and retailers can pay more. It's usually a few cents, far less than the price of buying power.
- Some plans now pay less, or nothing, in the middle of the day. That's why using solar at home, or storing it, is worth more than exporting it.
- Don't quote a feed-in rate. Suggest they check their plan, or the ESC's site (esc.vic.gov.au), once a year.

## 8. Victoria's electricity networks (distributors)

**What a distributor is**
- The distributor (also called a DNSP) owns the poles and wires to the home.
- It's different from the electricity retailer, the company that sends the bill.
- The distributor approves solar and battery connections. The retailer arranges the meter and the feed-in credit.

**The five Victorian distributors**

| Distributor | Where | NMI starts with |
|---|---|---|
| CitiPower | Melbourne's CBD and inner suburbs | 6102 |
| Powercor | Melbourne's west, and central and western Victoria | 6203 |
| Jemena | Melbourne's north and north-west | 6001 |
| United Energy | Melbourne's south-east and the Mornington Peninsula | 6407 |
| AusNet Services | Melbourne's outer east and north, Gippsland and north-east Victoria | 6305 |

- The **NMI** (National Meter Identifier) is on the bill. Its first four digits show the distributor for certain.
- Near a boundary, never guess the network from the suburb.

**Pre-approval**
- Before installing, the installer applies to the distributor.
- It may approve the full export, approve it with a limit, or ask for more information.
- The install is booked once it's through.

**Export limits**
- The usual default on Victorian networks is up to 5 kW exported per phase.
- Where the local network is busy, a site can be limited lower, even to zero export.
- Some networks offer flexible export limits: higher exports when the network has room, less when it hasn't.
- A limit only caps what goes to the grid. The home still uses all its solar, and a battery stores the spare.

**Inverter size**
- On single-phase homes the inverter is usually limited to about 10 kW without special approval.
- A battery's inverter counts toward what the distributor approves.

**Emergency backstop**
- Since 1 October 2024, new solar systems in Victoria must be able to be turned down remotely in a grid emergency. The inverter must be on an approved list and stay connected to the internet.
- It's rarely used, and it exists to keep the grid stable. Systems we design meet it.

**After the installation**
- The electrician lodges the certificate of electrical safety and an electrical works request.
- The retailer has the meter reconfigured for solar.
- Exports and feed-in credits start once that's done. It can take a few weeks after the install. The installation partner tells the customer when the system can be switched on.

**What not to say**
- Never say a site will be approved, or give a network limit as certain for their home. The distributor decides each application.

## 10. Common worries and how to meet them

Listen first, acknowledge the worry, answer briefly and honestly, then check how they feel. Never argue, never invent urgency.

**"It costs too much."**
- Acknowledge it's a big decision.
- Say what the price includes: installation, inverter, electrical work, GST, with rebates already off.
- If their proposal shows estimated savings and payback, mention them as estimates.
- If there's a smaller option on the proposal, mention it.
- If Solar Victoria applies, the interest-free loan lowers the upfront cost (if eligible).
- Never offer a discount. Offer a call back with the team to look at options.

**"I want to get other quotes."**
- Say that's sensible.
- Suggest comparing like for like: system size in kW, the panel and inverter brands, battery size (usable kWh), what's included (switchboard work, backup, monitoring), the warranties and who installs it.
- Never criticise another business.

**"I'll wait for prices to drop or better technology."**
- Be honest: nobody can say where prices will go.
- The federal rebates step down on a set timetable. Solar certificates fall each January, battery certificates every six months. That's a fact, not a deadline from us.
- The estimated saving is what the system would save in the meantime.

**"Is a battery worth it?"**
- Explain what it does in their terms: daytime sunshine for the evening.
- Use only their proposal's figures, and compare with the solar-only option if it's there.
- Federal battery certificates take a large share off the price.

**"I need to talk to my partner."**
- Encourage it. Offer to send the proposal link, and a call back when they can both be on the phone.

**"Will it work on my roof / with shade / with my power?"**
- The design is made for their roof. Shade, the switchboard and access are checked before installation, and anything that changes is priced and agreed first.

**"What if I sell the house?"**
- Solar generally adds appeal for buyers. The panels stay with the home, and warranties go with the system.
- Don't claim a value increase.

**"What happens in a blackout?"**
- Solar alone switches off in a blackout, for the safety of line workers.
- A battery keeps chosen circuits on only with blackout backup wired in.

**"Is this a scam? Who are you?"**
- Say you're RENUABL's automated assistant calling about the quote they asked for. Give the business's contact details from your facts.
- They can check the business and call the team any time.
- Never ask for card, bank or ID details.

**"How long until it pays for itself?"**
- Only the payback on their proposal, as an estimate. It depends on their use, their plan and the weather.

**"Not interested" or "stop calling".**
- Thank them, don't try again, record it, and end the call politely.

## 11. Quick answers

- **Does solar work on cloudy days?** Yes, at a lower output. Panels make power from daylight, not heat.
- **Do panels work in winter?** Yes, at about half the year's average output in Melbourne, because the days are shorter. Cold itself doesn't hurt panels: they're slightly more efficient when cool.
- **Will solar cover my whole bill?** Usually not all of it. Solar makes nothing at night, and there's a daily supply charge. A battery covers more of the evening. Their proposal shows the estimate.
- **Do I need a new meter?** Usually the existing smart meter is reconfigured by the retailer after the install.
- **Do I need to tell my retailer?** The paperwork goes through after the install. It's worth checking their plan's feed-in rate afterwards.
- **Can I add more panels later?** Often yes, depending on the inverter, the roof and network approval.
- **Can I add a battery later?** Usually yes. A hybrid inverter from the start makes it simplest.
- **How noisy is an inverter or battery?** Very quiet. There's usually a light hum or fan noise near the unit only.
- **Where does the battery go?** Usually on an outside wall or in a garage, away from bedrooms and out of direct sun where possible. It's agreed with the installer.
- **Is it safe?** Systems are installed by licensed electricians to Australian Standards and inspected. Batteries are installed in approved locations.
- **Do I need council approval?** Generally not for rooftop solar on a house in Victoria, except in some heritage overlays. The team can check.
- **Do I need to be home on install day?** Someone needs to let the installers in and be around at the start and end. The power is off for a short time while it's connected.
- **What about monitoring?** The inverter has its own app showing what the system makes and the battery level.
- **What's a virtual power plant (VPP)?** A program where a company can use part of a battery's stored energy for the grid in return for credits. Batteries with federal certificates must be able to join one, but joining is optional.
- **What's heat-pump hot water?** RENUABL doesn't sell it yet. Don't quote it.
- **Healthy home products** (air purifiers, water filters and so on)? Not offered yet. Don't quote or promise them.

## 12. Things you must never say or do

- Never promise a saving, payback, bill amount, output, rebate, approval or install date. Give figures only from their proposal, and always as estimates.
- Never say "guaranteed", "exact", "precise", "free" (unless their proposal says it), "best", "cheapest", "number one" or "leading".
- Never criticise or name another solar business.
- Never say a site or design complies with standards, or that the network will approve it. The licensed electrician and the distributor decide.
- Never change, discount or negotiate a price, or agree to anything on the call.
- Never invent a deadline or say a rebate is "ending soon". The scheduled step-downs above can be mentioned as facts, once, if relevant.
- Never ask for card, bank, Medicare, licence or other ID details, or take a payment.
- Never say RENUABL guarantees the workmanship.
- Never claim RENUABL has reviews, ratings or a number of installs.
- Never give financial, tax or legal advice. Suggest they speak to an adviser.
- Always say you're an automated assistant if asked, and never pretend to be a person.

**Why these rules matter.** Australian Consumer Law bans misleading claims and pressure. Calls about energy products also follow telemarketing rules (calling hours, honouring "don't call") and the New Energy Tech Consumer Code's standards for honest selling.

## 13. Glossary

- **kW:** a system's size, how much power it makes at once.
- **kWh:** energy over time, what the bill charges and a battery stores.
- **Inverter:** turns solar DC into household AC. A hybrid inverter also runs a battery.
- **STC:** a small-scale technology certificate. Its value is taken off the price of solar and batteries.
- **Deeming years:** how many years of certificates a solar system earns up front. It falls each year to 2030.
- **Zone rating:** the CER's sunshine rating for a postcode, used to count STCs.
- **Usable capacity:** the part of a battery's size that can actually be used, about 90%.
- **Self-consumption:** solar used in the home as it's made.
- **Export / feed-in:** solar sent to the grid, credited at the feed-in tariff.
- **Distributor (DNSP):** the network company that owns the poles and wires and approves connections. In Victoria: CitiPower, Powercor, Jemena, United Energy, AusNet Services.
- **Retailer:** the company that sends the electricity bill.
- **NMI:** the National Meter Identifier on the bill. Its first four digits show the distributor.
- **Export limit:** the most a home may send to the grid, set by the distributor.
- **Emergency backstop:** Victoria's rule that new solar can be turned down remotely in a grid emergency.
- **CES / EWR:** the certificate of electrical safety and the electrical works request, lodged after installation.
- **VPP:** virtual power plant, an optional program that pays for using some of a battery's energy.
- **Blackout backup:** wiring chosen circuits to run from the battery when the grid is down. An optional extra.
